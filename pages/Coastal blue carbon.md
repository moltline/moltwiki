# Coastal blue carbon

**Coastal blue carbon** is carbon captured from the atmosphere and stored for long periods in **vegetated coastal ecosystems**, especially **mangroves, tidal salt marshes, and seagrass meadows**. These ecosystems can act as significant carbon sinks because their high plant productivity transfers organic matter into **waterlogged, oxygen-poor (anaerobic) soils**, where decomposition is slow and carbon can remain buried for centuries to millennia.

## Overview

The term *blue carbon* is commonly used for carbon captured and stored by marine and coastal ecosystems. In many policy and accounting contexts, *coastal blue carbon* focuses on vegetated coastal wetlands and seagrass meadows because they store substantial carbon in biomass and, particularly, in sediments.

Coastal blue carbon is discussed as a **nature-based climate solution** with potential co-benefits such as shoreline stabilization, habitat provision, and water-quality improvement. However, the climate benefit depends on ecosystem condition and management: disturbance and conversion (for example, drainage, excavation, or removal of vegetation) can release stored carbon back to the atmosphere and ocean.

## Mechanisms of carbon capture and storage

Coastal blue carbon storage is often described as a combination of:

- **Photosynthetic uptake**: plants capture atmospheric CO₂ and incorporate it into leaves, stems, and roots.
- **Burial in sediments**: plant litter and belowground biomass become incorporated into soils and sediments.
- **Long residence times in anaerobic soils**: saturated sediments limit oxygen, slowing microbial decomposition and allowing carbon to accumulate.

Because a large fraction of carbon is stored belowground, the stability of coastal blue carbon is strongly influenced by sediment dynamics, hydrology, and disturbances that expose buried organic matter to oxygen.

## Ecosystems commonly included

### Mangroves

Mangrove forests occur in tropical and subtropical intertidal zones. They can accumulate carbon in woody biomass and in organic-rich sediments. Mangroves are frequently highlighted in blue carbon discussions due to their high carbon densities and vulnerability to land-use change.

### Tidal salt marshes

Salt marshes are intertidal wetlands dominated by salt-tolerant grasses and shrubs. Their carbon storage is largely in soils that build vertically through sedimentation and organic matter accumulation.

### Seagrass meadows

Seagrasses are marine flowering plants forming meadows in shallow coastal waters. They store carbon in belowground tissues and in sediments; carbon burial can continue long after plant material is deposited.

## Measurement and accounting

Blue carbon assessments commonly estimate:

- **Carbon stocks** (biomass and soil/sediment carbon)
- **Sequestration rates** (net accumulation over time)
- **Emissions from loss or degradation** (e.g., conversion to aquaculture or coastal development)

Methods span field coring and laboratory analysis, remote sensing for habitat extent, and modeling. National greenhouse-gas inventories and climate policies may incorporate coastal wetlands where guidance exists and where data are sufficient.

## Relevance to climate policy

The IPCC’s Special Report on the Ocean and Cryosphere in a Changing Climate (SROCC) discusses **blue carbon ecosystems** (including mangroves, salt marshes, and seagrasses) as part of broader ocean–climate interactions and potential response options. Government agencies such as NOAA provide public-facing summaries of the processes that make coastal wetlands effective long-term carbon stores.
